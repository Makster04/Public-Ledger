# Explainer
Methodology

This scorecard measures how members of Congress have aligned on a set of Palestine-related bills, resolutions, votes, and public letters. Each item is coded as either more supportive of Palestinian rights and humanitarian protections or more supportive of policies opposed to those goals. Members receive positive points for actions aligned with the former and negative points for actions aligned with the latter.

To reflect the relative significance of different kinds of actions, the scorecard uses weighted scoring:

3 points for sponsoring or cosponsoring legislation or resolutions
2 points for voting on legislation or resolutions
1 point for signing public letters

Each member’s total score is the sum of these item-level positions, adjusted for when they were in Congress. Higher scores indicate a more consistently pro-Palestine record; lower scores indicate a more consistently anti-Palestine record.

The scorecard is based on publicly available congressional records and public source materials and considers legislation and letters after October 7. Bill and resolution sponsorship and cosponsorship are drawn from official Congress tracking sources. Vote data is drawn from official roll call records. Letter signatories are compiled from publicly released member offices’ letters, congressional press releases, and other public documentation identifying signers. Items are included only when there is a publicly verifiable basis for coding member participation or position.

Grades are assigned separately for the House and Senate based on the distribution of scores within each chamber and are adjusted again depending on when the Member was sworn into office. This means a member’s letter grade reflects how their record compares to others in the same chamber, rather than against a single pooled scale.

This methodology is designed to balance clarity, consistency, and interpretability. It necessarily simplifies some distinctions among items: not every bill, resolution, vote, or letter carries the same substantive importance, and some procedural votes may be less informative than final passage or sponsorship decisions. The scorecard therefore should be understood as a structured comparative measure of congressional alignment, not a definitive judgment on any member’s overall leadership or advocacy.
