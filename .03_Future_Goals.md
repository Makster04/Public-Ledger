# Public Ledger: Dark Money & Legislative Influence

### Proposal for Connecting Political Funding, Interest Groups, Legislation, and Lawmakers' Actions

## Overview

The strongest way to improve **Public Ledger** would be to connect **political funding → interest groups → specific legislation → lawmakers' actions**, while clearly distinguishing dark-money funding from publicly disclosed contributions.

Public Ledger already has much of the infrastructure needed. The major opportunity is to make financial and legislative relationships more specific, particularly at the individual bill, amendment, and committee level.

The goal is to move beyond simply identifying who financially supports politicians and toward examining **how political organizations' documented interests relate to elected officials' legislative behavior**.

This would allow voters to investigate who financially supports an elected official, what legislation those organizations publicly advocate for, and whether the politician's actions align with those positions.

Importantly, financial support or a matching vote should never automatically be presented as evidence of corruption, coordination, or a purchased political decision.

---

## 1. Build a Money-to-Legislation Tracker

Instead of simply displaying how much financial support a politician received, Public Ledger could allow visitors to investigate:

- Which organizations supported or opposed a politician's election.
- Where those organizations received their funding.
- Whether the original funding sources are publicly disclosed.
- Which legislative issues and bills those organizations support or oppose.
- Whether politicians sponsored, amended, or voted on those bills.
- Whether their legislative actions matched the organizations' documented positions.
- Whether relevant financial activity occurred before or after legislative decisions.
- How the politician's actions compare with other members of their party.

### Proposed Financial-to-Legislative Relationship

**Stage 1: Original Funding Source**

- Individual, corporation, organization, or unidentified donor.
- Disclosure status: disclosed, partially disclosed, or unknown.

↓

**Stage 2: Nonprofit / Political Organization**

- Receives and distributes funding.
- May engage in political advocacy, lobbying, or election-related activities.
- Its original donors may not always be publicly identifiable.

↓

**Stage 3: PAC / Super PAC / Independent Spending**

- Documented financial support for or opposition to candidates.
- Direct campaign contributions and independent expenditures are tracked separately.
- Super PAC election spending must not be misrepresented as money received by the candidate.

↓

**Stage 4: Elected Official**

- Identifies politicians whose campaigns received contributions or whose elections were affected by independent spending.
- Links the politician to their legislative responsibilities and recorded activities.

↓

**Stage 5: Legislation and Bills**

- Bill sponsorship and cosponsorship.
- Amendments and committee activity.
- Recorded votes.
- Documented support or opposition.
- Legislative outcomes.

**Important distinction:** These stages describe possible relationships rather than a single proven chain of influence. Each connection must be independently supported by evidence.

Outside spending is not a campaign contribution, and observed agreement between a politician and an organization does not prove that the organization influenced the politician.

The missing component is a **unified, evidence-backed relationship between a funding organization, its legislative interests, and the actions of an elected official**.

## 3. Five Core Improvements

### A. Funding Source Transparency

Trace publicly documented financial relationships among donors, nonprofits, political committees, PACs, and Super PACs.

**Proposed capabilities:**

- Identify organizations using FEC committee IDs and nonprofit EINs.
- Track publicly reported transfers and contributions.
- Distinguish campaign contributions from independent expenditures.
- Identify the last publicly verifiable source of funding.
- Mark original donor identities as disclosed, partially disclosed, or unknown.
- Identify financial intermediaries without automatically treating all intermediaries as dark-money organizations.
- Provide access to the underlying FEC, IRS, and organizational records.

**Objective:** Allow users to follow political money as far as the public records reliably permit.

The system must not speculate about donors whose identities cannot be established.

### B. Legislative Interest Matching

Identify which specific bills, amendments, provisions, and legislative issues political organizations publicly support or oppose.

**Potential sources:**

- Federal lobbying disclosure reports, including LD-2 filings.
- Official organizational statements and policy letters.
- Congressional committee testimony.
- Interest-group legislative scorecards.
- Public advocacy campaigns.
- Official congressional records.

**Proposed capabilities:**

- Match organizations to specific bill identifiers.
- Record documented support or opposition.
- Distinguish lobbying on a bill from explicitly supporting or opposing it.
- Identify the particular provisions an organization advocates for.
- Record when the organization expressed its position.
- Attach original supporting sources.

For example, an organization could lobby on healthcare legislation while supporting some provisions and opposing others.

Public Ledger should distinguish those positions rather than assuming the organization supports or opposes the entire bill.

### C. Funding and Legislation Timeline

Create a chronological timeline that combines financial activity with legislative developments.

**Events to display:**

- Campaign contributions.
- Reported nonprofit transfers.
- Super PAC independent expenditures.
- Lobbying disclosures.
- Public policy statements.
- Bill introductions.
- Sponsorships and cosponsorships.
- Committee hearings and votes.
- Legislative amendments.
- Final congressional votes.

**Objective:** Help users understand the timing of political financing and legislative activity.

For example, visitors could investigate whether documented organizational spending occurred before or after a politician sponsored legislation concerning that organization's interests.

The system should flag notable timing relationships for further investigation without claiming that one event caused another.

### D. Legislative Alignment Analysis

Compare a politician's legislative behavior with an organization's documented legislative positions.

**Key measurements:**

- Agreement on recorded votes.
- Sponsorship or cosponsorship of supported bills.
- Support or opposition through legislative amendments.
- Relevant committee activity.
- Changes in legislative position over time.
- Comparison with the politician's party.
- Comparison with politicians holding similar ideological positions or committee assignments.

Where an organization has no publicly documented position, Public Ledger should display:

**No documented position**

It should never assume an organization's position based solely on its industry, political affiliations, or funding patterns.

### E. Evidence and Disclosure Labels

Every relationship should communicate both the supporting evidence and its limitations.

**Suggested evidence categories:**

- **Verified financial transaction:** Supported by an official transaction or filing.
- **Documented legislative position:** Supported by an organizational statement or other reliable evidence.
- **Documented legislative agreement:** A politician's action matches an organization's explicitly stated position.
- **Timing correlation:** Financial and legislative events occurred within a defined period.
- **Requires further review:** A potential relationship exists, but evidence is incomplete.
- **Unknown:** Available records do not establish the relevant connection.

All findings should link directly to their original sources.

A funding connection, organizational policy position, and politician's legislative action should be treated as separate facts unless evidence establishes a more specific relationship.

## 5. Technical Implementation in Public Ledger

Public Ledger would not need to be rebuilt from scratch.

Relevant existing components include:

- `public/influence.js`
- `scripts/build-funders.mjs`
- `public/research.json`
- FEC campaign finance records.
- PAC and Super PAC expenditure records.
- Congress.gov legislative and voting data.
- Lobbying disclosures.
- ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer.

The proposed approach is to extend the existing data infrastructure with four related datasets.

### Proposed Data Structure

| New Dataset              | Information Stored                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `funding_links`          | Source organization, receiving organization, amount, date, transaction type, disclosure status, and source URL.         |
| `group_bill_positions`   | Organization ID, bill ID, legislative provision, documented support/opposition, position date, and evidence URL.        |
| `legislative_actions`    | Politician ID, bill ID, action type, date, recorded position, and official source.                                      |
| `money_bill_connections` | Linked organization, bill, politician, documented financial pathway, legislative relationships, and supporting records. |

Each organization should have stable identifiers and verified aliases to prevent accidentally linking unrelated organizations with similar names.

Bills should use official congressional identifiers, including the relevant Congress number, to distinguish legislation introduced in different sessions.

The `money_bill_connections` dataset should be generated from the other datasets rather than treated as an independent source of factual claims.

### How the Analysis Would Work

**Step 1: Identify Financial Relationships**

Collect publicly reported contributions, nonprofit transfers, and independent expenditures.

Trace funding only as far as reliable public records allow.

**Step 2: Identify Legislative Interests**

Match organizations to bills and provisions they have explicitly supported, opposed, or lobbied on.

Separate direct evidence of a position from general lobbying activity.

**Step 3: Identify Legislative Actions**

Retrieve lawmakers' sponsorships, cosponsorships, committee participation, amendments, and recorded votes.

**Step 4: Connect the Records**

Match organizations, politicians, legislation, and relevant dates using verified identifiers and relationships.

**Step 5: Evaluate the Evidence**

Distinguish documented financial connections, direct legislative agreements, broader policy relationships, and findings requiring additional research.

**Step 6: Present the Findings**

Display financial relationships, legislative activity, timelines, and original sources through a unified interface.

### Additional Data Quality Requirements

- Prevent duplicate financial transactions and legislative events.
- Preserve the original data source and collection date.
- Distinguish the date an event occurred from the date it was publicly reported.
- Avoid counting the same money twice as it passes through intermediaries.
- Distinguish campaign receipts from independent expenditures.
- Record when an organization publicly expressed its legislative position.
- Preserve missing data as unknown rather than interpreting it as zero activity.
- Identify amended filings and update previously reported information.
- Clearly distinguish planned analysis features from fully implemented calculations.

**Important:** A policy statement published after a congressional vote should not automatically be treated as evidence of what the organization wanted before that vote.

## 7. Statistical Analysis: Comparing Politicians Against a Baseline

One particularly valuable improvement would be comparing documented legislative agreement with political and institutional baselines.

### Why Agreement Percentages Alone Can Be Misleading

Suppose a politician votes in agreement with a financial supporter's publicly stated legislative positions **85% of the time**.

That percentage alone provides limited information.

Consider two hypothetical situations:

**Scenario A:**

- Politician's agreement with the organization: 85%.
- Agreement among other members of the politician's party: 90%.

The politician's agreement is actually lower than the party baseline.

**Scenario B:**

- Politician's agreement with the organization: 85%.
- Agreement among other members of the politician's party: 40%.

The politician's agreement is substantially higher than the party baseline.

The same 85% agreement rate means something very different in these two situations.

Neither scenario establishes that financial support caused the politician's behavior.

### Proposed Statistical Improvements

Public Ledger already uses related logic in its Donor Loyalty analysis.

That framework could be expanded to incorporate:

**1. Party Baseline Comparisons**

Compare a politician's voting agreement with an organization against how their party voted on the same legislative questions.

**2. Ideological Comparisons**

Compare politicians with similar ideological positions to account for preexisting policy preferences.

**3. Committee-Based Comparisons**

Consider committee memberships and legislative responsibilities, since politicians serving on relevant committees often engage more frequently with the affected industries and organizations.

**4. Longitudinal Analysis**

Examine changes in financial relationships and legislative behavior over multiple congressional sessions.

**5. Statistical Relationship Analysis**

Use appropriate regression models and other statistical methods to investigate associations between financial activity and legislative behavior while accounting for relevant confounding factors.

Potential explanatory variables could include:

- Political party.
- Ideological position.
- Committee membership.
- State or district.
- Election cycle.
- Legislative issue.
- Historical voting patterns.
- Financial support received.
- Independent spending benefiting or opposing the candidate.

The analysis must also address the possibility that organizations support politicians because those politicians already share their policy positions.

**Even a statistically significant association does not establish that financial contributions or independent spending caused a legislative decision.**

Statistical results should remain separate from the existing political scorecards unless a new methodology is explicitly developed, validated, and approved.

## 9. What Public Ledger Would Ultimately Help Answer

The platform would go beyond simply reporting how much money was involved in an election.

It could help the public investigate:

- Who financially supported a politician's election, directly or independently?
- Which original funding sources can be identified, and which remain undisclosed?
- Through which organizations did publicly documented money flow?
- What legislation did those organizations advocate for?
- Which bills, amendments, and legislative provisions were involved?
- What actions did the elected official take concerning those bills?
- How closely did those actions align with documented organizational positions?
- Did financial or legislative activity change around particular political events?
- Were these relationships consistent with the politician's party or ideological positions?
- Which relationships are supported by reliable evidence, and which remain uncertain?

### The Larger Vision

**Public Ledger could become a comprehensive political transparency and research platform connecting political money to the legislative process, from documented financial transactions and outside spending to individual bills, amendments, committee actions, and votes.**

The most important distinction would be separating three fundamental questions:

**1. Who funded the political activity?**

Identify publicly documented financial relationships and clearly indicate where original funding sources remain unknown.

**2. What legislation did the organization seek to influence?**

Examine documented lobbying activity, policy positions, public statements, and legislative advocacy.

**3. What did the elected official actually do?**

Analyze sponsorships, cosponsorships, amendments, committee activity, votes, and other legislative actions.

Where evidence establishes a relationship, Public Ledger should present that relationship with its supporting records.

Where evidence is incomplete, the platform should explain what remains unknown rather than speculate.

### Final Objective

The goal is not to presume that financial support proves political corruption.

It is to make relationships between political financing, organizational interests, and legislative decisions **transparent, measurable, verifiable, and accessible to the public**.

By combining campaign finance records, dark-money transparency, lobbying disclosures, legislative activity, and statistical comparisons, Public Ledger could provide a more comprehensive picture of the financial interests surrounding American politics.

**Follow the money. Examine the legislation. Compare the actions. Show the evidence.**

# Connecting Dark Money to Legislation in Public Ledger

The strongest way to improve Public Ledger would be to connect **political funding → interest groups → specific legislation → lawmakers' actions**, with dark-money funding clearly distinguished from publicly disclosed contributions.

## 1. Build a Money-to-Legislation Tracker

Instead of simply showing how much financial support a politician received, Public Ledger could let visitors investigate **which organizations supported their election, what legislation those organizations wanted, and what the politician actually did**.

**Original funding source**

Disclosed, partially disclosed, or unknown

↓

**Nonprofit / political organization**

Identifiable intermediary and public activities

↓

**Super PAC / independent election spending**

Documented candidate support or opposition

↓

**Elected official**

Candidate who benefited from or was opposed by spending

↓

**Legislation and bills**

Sponsorship, amendments, committee work, and votes

*Conceptual relationship map. These are separate, evidence-dependent connections—not proof that the original funder directed the lawmaker's actions.*

This would answer more useful questions than simply "Who donated?"

For example:

**The critical distinction:** outside election spending is not a contribution to a candidate's campaign. It is legally defined as independent of the campaign, so Public Ledger must preserve that distinction.

## 7. An important statistical improvement: compare politicians against a baseline

This is where Public Ledger could become particularly useful for political research.

Suppose a politician votes in agreement with a financial supporter's publicly stated legislative positions **85% of the time**.

That percentage alone doesn't tell us much.

If **90% of the politician's party voted the same way** on those bills, the observed agreement might primarily reflect shared political ideology rather than anything distinctive about that politician's funding relationships.

But if **only 40% of the politician's party agreed** with those positions, the difference could warrant closer examination.

You already have a related approach in the repository's **Donor Loyalty** calculations. I would expand that logic to bill sponsorships, amendments, and committee actions, while keeping the comparison descriptive rather than treating it as proof of influence.

For more rigorous analysis, Public Ledger could eventually compare legislators with similar party affiliations, ideologies, committee assignments, and voting histories to investigate whether financial support is associated with differences in their legislative behavior.

**Even a statistically significant association would not, by itself, establish that the funding caused the legislative action.**
