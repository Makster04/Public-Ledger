# Connecting Dark Money to Legislation in Public Ledger

The strongest way to improve Public Ledger would be to connect **political funding → interest groups → specific legislation → lawmakers' actions**, with dark-money funding clearly distinguished from publicly disclosed contributions.

After reviewing your repository, you already have much of the infrastructure needed. The major opportunity is to make the relationships more specific, particularly at the individual bill and amendment level.

## 1. Build a Money-to-Legislation Tracker

Instead of simply showing how much financial support a politician received, Public Ledger could let visitors investigate **which organizations supported their election, what legislation those organizations wanted, and what the politician actually did**.

**Original funding source**

Disclosed, partially disclosed, or unknown

↓

**Nonprofit / political organization**

Identifiable intermediary and public activities

↓

**Super PAC / independent election spending**

Documented candidate support or opposition

↓

**Elected official**

Candidate who benefited from or was opposed by spending

↓

**Legislation and bills**

Sponsorship, amendments, committee work, and votes

*Conceptual relationship map. These are separate, evidence-dependent connections—not proof that the original funder directed the lawmaker's actions.*

This would answer more useful questions than simply "Who donated?"

For example:

- Which organizations independently spent money supporting this politician?
- Which bills did those organizations publicly support or oppose?
- Did the politician sponsor, amend, or vote on those bills?
- Did the politician's actions match the organizations' documented positions?
- Did the activity occur before or after a key legislative decision?
- How did that politician's actions compare with other members of their party?

**The critical distinction:** outside election spending is not a contribution to a candidate's campaign. It is legally defined as independent of the campaign, so Public Ledger must preserve that distinction.

| Donation Timing Flags | Incorporate independent spending, bill introductions, committee actions, and amendments |

The key missing component is a **unified, documented relationship between a funding organization, its legislative objectives, and an elected official's bill-level actions**.

## 3. Five additions I would prioritize

### A. Funding Source Transparency

Track money across nonprofits, PACs, Super PACs, and other reporting entities using FEC committee IDs and nonprofit EINs. Identify the last publicly verifiable source, rather than guessing who an anonymous donor is.

### B. Legislative Interest Matching

Record which specific bills and provisions an organization publicly supports or opposes. Use lobbying disclosures, official organizational statements, legislative testimony, and position letters.

Federal LD-2 lobbying reports include specific issues and applicable bill numbers, making them particularly valuable.

### C. Funding and Legislation Timeline

Place verified financial transactions, independent expenditures, lobbying filings, sponsorships, committee actions, and votes on a single chronological timeline.

This helps show whether financial and legislative activity occurred close together, without interpreting timing alone as causation.

### D. Legislative Alignment Analysis

Compare a politician's documented actions with an organization's explicit position on the same bill or provision. Also show historical positions, party patterns, committee responsibilities, and disagreements.

Where an organization's position is unknown, display **No documented position**, not an assumed match.

### E. Evidence and Disclosure Labels

Distinguish confirmed transactions, documented policy positions, matching legislative actions, and correlations that warrant further review. Every connection should link to its original records.

This would make the information much easier for the public to understand while keeping documented facts separate from suggested relationships.

## 5. How I would implement this in your GitHub project

You wouldn't need to rebuild Public Ledger from scratch.

Your code already includes `public/influence.js`, `scripts/build-funders.mjs`, `public/research.json`, FEC funding information, and congressional bill and voting data.

I'd extend that infrastructure with four related datasets:

| New dataset | Information to store |
|---|---|
| `funding_links` | Source organization, receiving organization, amount, date, record URL, donor disclosure status |
| `group_bill_positions` | Organization ID, bill ID, specific provision, support/opposition, evidence URL |
| `legislative_actions` | Politician ID, bill ID, action type, action date, recorded position |
| `money_bill_connections` | Linked organization, bill, politician, financial pathway, matched actions, supporting records |

Each organization should have stable identifiers and verified aliases to prevent accidentally linking unrelated organizations with similar names. Each bill should also use its official congressional identifier.

Importantly, `money_bill_connections` should be a **derived dataset** generated from the other three, rather than an independent source of claims.

### How the analysis would work

1. **Identify financial relationships.** Collect publicly reported contributions, nonprofit transfers, and independent expenditures. Trace the money only as far as reliable records allow.

2. **Identify legislative interests.** Match each organization to bills it has explicitly supported, opposed, or lobbied on. Do not assume a position based solely on its industry.

3. **Identify legislative actions.** Retrieve politicians' bill sponsorships, cosponsorships, committee activity, amendments, and recorded votes.

4. **Connect the records.** Match the same organization, politician, bill, and relevant dates to establish documented relationships.

5. **Evaluate the evidence.** Distinguish a direct legislative position match from a broader policy-interest connection or a relationship that requires additional investigation.

6. **Display the findings.** Present the funding trail, legislative actions, timeline, and original sources together.

One additional improvement: record *when an organization publicly expressed its position*. A statement published after a congressional vote should not automatically be treated as proof of what the organization wanted before that vote.

## 6. The feature I would introduce: Money & Legislative Influence

I would make this a dedicated section under a politician's existing **Money & Influence Analysis** tab, rather than creating an entirely separate scoring system.

It could contain three connected views.

### Funding Network

An interactive diagram showing disclosed donors, nonprofits, Super PACs, lobbying organizations, and their documented financial relationships. Unidentified funding sources should be visibly marked as unknown.

### Legislation Connections

A searchable table of bills, organizations' documented positions, politicians' actions, and supporting evidence. Visitors could filter by healthcare, housing, taxation, defense, energy, and other policy categories.

### Money & Policy Timeline

A chronological display of financial activity and legislative developments, allowing visitors to see which events preceded or followed major votes or bill amendments.

## 7. An important statistical improvement: compare politicians against a baseline

This is where Public Ledger could become particularly useful for political research.

Suppose a politician votes in agreement with a financial supporter's publicly stated legislative positions **85% of the time**.

That percentage alone doesn't tell us much.

If **90% of the politician's party voted the same way** on those bills, the observed agreement might primarily reflect shared political ideology rather than anything distinctive about that politician's funding relationships.

But if **only 40% of the politician's party agreed** with those positions, the difference could warrant closer examination.

You already have a related approach in the repository's **Donor Loyalty** calculations. I would expand that logic to bill sponsorships, amendments, and committee actions, while keeping the comparison descriptive rather than treating it as proof of influence.

For more rigorous analysis, Public Ledger could eventually compare legislators with similar party affiliations, ideologies, committee assignments, and voting histories to investigate whether financial support is associated with differences in their legislative behavior.

**Even a statistically significant association would not, by itself, establish that the funding caused the legislative action.**

## 9. What Public Ledger would ultimately help answer

The platform would go beyond reporting the amount of money involved in an election.

It could help the public investigate:

- Who financially supported a politician's election, directly or independently?
- Which original funding sources can be identified, and which remain undisclosed?
- What legislation did those organizations publicly advocate for?
- What did the politician do concerning that legislation?
- How closely did those actions align with documented organizational positions?
- Were there unusual changes in funding or legislative behavior around particular events?
- Are observed relationships consistent with party ideology, or do they differ from relevant comparisons?

### The larger vision

**Public Ledger could become a public research platform connecting political money to the legislative process, from documented financial transactions to specific bills, amendments, and votes.**

The most valuable distinction would be separating three questions:

**Who funded the political activity? What legislation did the organization seek to influence? What did the elected official actually do?**

Where the evidence supports a connection, Public Ledger would show it. Where the evidence is incomplete, it would explain what remains unknown.

That approach would allow Public Ledger to investigate dark-money networks and their relationships to legislation without overstating what the public records can prove.
