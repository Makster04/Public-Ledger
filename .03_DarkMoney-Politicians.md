# How Public Ledger connects dark money to politicians

## What dark money is

Election spending whose original funders aren't public. It usually starts with a nonprofit that doesn't have to name its donors: a "social welfare" group (501(c)(4)) or a trade association (501(c)(6)). The nonprofit either spends on ads itself, or gives to a super PAC whose report then names only the nonprofit as the donor.

Public Ledger follows the money to the last group that reports it. It can't name the donors behind that group, and it never guesses.

## What the site collects for each politician

- **Campaign money (FEC):** total raised, gifts by size, PAC money by type, gifts that groups like AIPAC collect and forward to the campaign, and joint fundraising.
- **Outside spending (FEC independent expenditures):** every group's spending for or against them. It isn't money the campaign received or controls.
- **Who the spenders are:**
  - **Super PACs** report their donors. The Super PACs page names their largest organization donors; people who give are added up.
  - **Groups that file with the FEC on their own, not as PACs** (FEC IDs starting with C9) report only gifts given for their ads, so most of their money's sources aren't public. The site flags them as "lists few donors".
  - The refresh looks up outside spenders in ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer and shows them as "Groups behind outside spending" on the profile.
- **Ads that named them near an election (FEC electioneering communications):** TV, radio and cable ads that name them within 30 days of a primary or 60 days of a general election, paid for by groups that aren't political committees. They needn't say "vote for", so they aren't outside spending, and the groups report only the donors who gave for these ads, so each is flagged "lists few donors". By group, with amounts and dates.
- **Corporations and unions telling their members how to vote (FEC communication costs):** what each spent telling its own members, executives or shareholders to vote for or against them, once it passes $2,000 an election.
- **Nonprofits' own tax returns (IRS Form 990, from the IRS's e-file downloads):** for the tracked groups' nonprofit arms, outside spenders that are nonprofits, groups that ran ads naming a candidate, and super PACs' largest organization donors (gifts of $100,000 or more), the newest return's totals, political spending, every super PAC and other political group it paid, its grants to other organizations, and which of the other nonprofits followed here gave it grants. Grants of $1 million or more to political nonprofits are followed one step further. Shown beside each group's name on the profiles, the bill pages and the Super PACs page, where a donor that's a nonprofit is marked "doesn't name its donors". A return's donor list is blacked out of public copies, so the trail stops at the last nonprofit.
- **Lobbyists' political gifts to them (LDA.gov LD-203):** gifts lobbyists and lobbying organizations report naming them as the one honored, in the current cycle's two years. Lobbyists are added up, never named; an organization's own gifts name the organization. Full profiles only.

## How each score connects

| Score | Connection |
|---|---|
| **Big Money Independence** | **Direct, but disclosed money only.** It scores their campaign money and holdings: 75% + Donor − PAC Penalty − PAC Reliance − Earmark Penalty − Stock Penalty − Trade Penalty + Bonuses + Pledges. Money groups collect and forward costs points ($50,000 costs 1). Outside spending, where dark money sits, is **not** counted. |
| **People First** | **Indirect.** A quarter of it is Corporation Compromised: 65% is how much more often they vote with the business groups that gave to the campaign than the rest of their party does, weighted by each group's money (25 points above the party scores 100%, with at least 5 compared votes). The other 35% is the profit side on bills those donors lobbied on. Money decides whose positions count; the score measures votes. |
| **Plays by the Rules** | **Accountability.** Trade Conflicts counts trades in donors' companies (25% of it). Legal Trouble counts campaign-finance cases, such as FEC enforcement matters and complaints about late disclosure. Taking outside support isn't itself a violation. |

**Others: Ideology, Keeps Their Word and Shows Up** use no money.

None of the six scores measures dark money.

## On the profile's Big Money Independence tab

- **The money map:** where the campaign's money comes from, by source and gift size.
- **Under "Shown, not counted in Big Money Independence":** the ads that named them near an election, corporations' and unions' messages to their members about them, lobbyists' gifts to them, the top donor organizations and the groups behind outside spending.
- **The seven Trackers:** Pro-Israel Lobby, Big Pharma + Insurance, Big Oil, Big Tech + AI, Crypto, Big Defense and Wall Street. Each shows the lobby's PAC checks, the gifts it forwarded, and its super PAC spending for or against them. They're shown, not counted.

## What the site can't show

- The original donors behind a nonprofit.
- Whether a group coordinated with a campaign.
- That money caused a vote.

People who give are added up, not named, except the donors the Trackers name by design.
