# How Public Ledger connects dark money to politicians

## What dark money is

Election spending whose original funders aren't public. It usually starts with a nonprofit that doesn't have to name its donors: a "social welfare" group (501(c)(4)) or a trade association (501(c)(6)). The nonprofit either spends on ads itself, or gives to a super PAC whose report then names only the nonprofit as the donor.

Public Ledger follows the money to the last group that reports it. It can't name the donors behind that group, and it never guesses.

## What the site collects for each politician

- **Campaign money (FEC):** total raised, gifts by size, PAC money by type, gifts that groups like AIPAC collect and forward to the campaign, and joint fundraising.
- **Outside spending (FEC independent expenditures):** every group's spending for or against them. It isn't money the campaign received or controls.
- **Who the spenders are:**
  - **Super PACs** report their donors. The Super PACs page names their largest organization donors; people who give are added up.
  - **Groups that file with the FEC on their own, not as PACs** (FEC IDs starting with C9) report only gifts given for their ads, so most of their money's sources aren't public. The site flags them as "lists few donors".
  - The refresh looks up outside spenders in ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer and shows them as "Groups behind outside spending" on the profile.

## How each score connects

| Score | Connection |
|---|---|
| **Big Money Independence** | **Direct, but disclosed money only.** It scores their campaign money and holdings: 75% + Donor − PAC Penalty − PAC Reliance − Earmark Penalty − Stock Penalty − Trade Penalty + Bonuses + Pledges. Money groups collect and forward costs points ($50,000 costs 1). Outside spending, where dark money sits, is **not** counted. |
| **People First** | **Indirect.** A quarter of it is Corporation Compromised: 65% how much more often they vote with the business groups that gave to the campaign or spent to help it than their party does, and 35% the profit side on bills those donors lobbied on. Money decides whose positions count; the score measures votes. |
| **Plays by the Rules** | **Accountability.** Trade Conflicts counts trades in donors' companies (25% of it). Legal Trouble counts campaign-finance cases, such as FEC enforcement matters and complaints about late disclosure. Taking outside support isn't itself a violation. |

**Others: Ideology, Keeps Their Word and Shows Up** use no money.

None of the six scores measures dark money.

## Other features on the profile

All on each politician's **Big Money Independence** tab. The money map (in "Evidence: where the money comes from") and the Trackers are on the tab itself. Vote Donor Alignment, Donor Loyalty, Timing flags and Stock in donors are in **Money & Influence Analysis**, inside the "Shown, not counted in Big Money Independence" section near the bottom; the link `#<id>-connections` (for example `#collins-connections`) opens it.

- **Vote Donor Alignment:** how often their votes went the way a donor with a public position wanted.
- **Donor Loyalty:** how much more often they vote their funders' way than the rest of their party does on the same votes. It's weighted by money, no funder carries more than half the weight, 25 points above the party scores 100%, and it needs 5 compared votes.
- **Timing flags:** among their top 20 donors, a group's donations rising or falling 50% within 6 months of a vote. For review; not scored.
- **Stock in donors**, the money map, and the seven Trackers (Pro-Israel Lobby, Big Pharma + Insurance, Big Oil, Big Tech + AI, Crypto, Big Defense, Wall Street) with their super PAC spending.

## What the site can't show

- The original donors behind a nonprofit.
- Whether a group coordinated with a campaign.
- That money caused a vote.

People who give are added up, not named, except the donors the Trackers name by design.
